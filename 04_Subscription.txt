Case (by Claude)

Subscription/SaaS — churn and NPS

Monthly churn fell from 4.5% to 3.2% in the same period NPS rose from +18 to +31. 
Leadership concludes "the NPS improvement is why churn dropped."
Question: what timing, survivorship, and cohort issues would make this a premature conclusion? 
What's the actual sequence you'd expect between an NPS-driving change and a churn change?-

Review (by Claude)

Strong points

1. Grounding the hypothesis in actual complaint content, not just the aggregate score. 
Checking which specific complaints disappeared from NPS verbatims and 
proposing that fixes to those issues are what's keeping people around is a reasonable attempt to find a mechanism, not just point at a number.

2. You correctly go looking for alternative explanations rather than stopping at the stakeholder's story. 
Seasonality, reactivation campaigns, and new feature adoption are all legitimate competing explanations, 
and checking seasonality against prior years' data reuses a habit you applied well in the taxi case.

3. The feature-adoption check (proportion of current base using it) is a sound first step toward 
testing whether a specific product change could plausibly account for a churn shift of this size.


Weak points / gaps

1. Survivorship bias is missing, and it's probably the single biggest risk here. 
NPS surveys are typically sent to currently active customers. 
If churn fell, the surviving customer pool this month mechanically contains more of the people who would have churned in a normal month but didn't (for any reason)
 — and if dissatisfied customers already left in earlier cycles, the remaining population is satisfied by composition, not because anyone's sentiment actually improved.
 This raises a real possibility you haven't considered: falling churn could be causing the NPS rise, 
not the other way around — exactly the reverse of what leadership concluded. You'd want to check whether NPS respondent composition shifted 
(are you surveying a different mix of tenure/plan customers than before?) and whether low-churn months historically also show NPS bumps for this same mechanical reason.

2. Cohort composition is also unaddressed. 
If a wave of new customers (who are both happier and less likely to churn in an early honeymoon period) 
are diluting the base, or if a historically high-churn cohort recently rolled off, both metrics would move together without any causal link between them
 — the same mix-shift logic you applied well in the e-commerce case (new/retained customer segmentation) but didn't transfer here. 
Worth checking NPS and churn broken out by cohort (signup vintage, plan tier, acquisition channel) before trusting the blended number.

3. Starting from "assume the correlation is causal and go find supporting evidence" risks confirmation bias. 
Hunting for which complaints disappeared is naturally going to surface something — product teams resolve complaints every month as routine work
 — so this approach can "confirm" almost any month's churn change after the fact. 
A stronger test would compare churn specifically among customers who filed those particular complaints vs. 
those who didn't, before and after the fix shipped, rather than a general narrative match. 
This step belongs after you've ruled out the lag/survivorship/cohort explanations, not before.

4. No magnitude check. Even if seasonality, campaigns, and the new feature are all real contributors, 
you haven't proposed how you'd quantify how much of the 4.5%→3.2% drop each one explains — without that, 
you can confirm several plausible stories are "present" while still not knowing if they're sufficient, 
or if a large unexplained residual remains.

5. The feature-adoption check needs a comparison group, not just a proportion. 
Knowing what % of the base uses the new feature doesn't tell you much by itself
 — you'd want churn rate among adopters vs. non-adopters, and ideally some adjustment for the fact that people
 who adopt new features early are often inherently lower-churn, engaged power users regardless of the feature itself (a selection effect, not a treatment effect).
