Case

Taxi segment — bonus earn/burn
Total bonus earn and burn across the platform dropped 15% month-over-month. 
The product team's first read: "the new bonus campaign in the taxi segment underperformed, so fewer bonuses were earned and burned." 
Taxi is one of several verticals (food delivery, grocery, micromobility) sharing the same wallet and bonus pool.
Question: is the taxi campaign really the driver, or could this be a segment-mix, seasonality, or total-turnover effect? 
What would you check first, second, third?

Answer

1- Firstly, if taxi segment earn and burn both decrease at the same time it shows there is problem on taxi segment itself. 
Because only earn or only burn decrease shows not segment but general problems.

Why - because less balance means less burn opportunity. However, here we see both burn and earn dropped

Review

Strong points
1. The earn-and-burn-together heuristic is genuinely useful. 
Separating "only burn dropped" (points to a spending-side constraint like low balance) from "both dropped together" 
(points to something upstream, shared by both) is a real diagnostic distinction. Most people wouldn't think to split it this way.

2. Turnover-before-campaign ordering respects base rates. 
Checking whether the underlying business activity moved before blaming a specific program is the correct instinct — 
campaigns are usually the last thing to check, not the first, because they're a small lever on a big base.

3. YoY comparison operationalizes "seasonality" instead of leaving it as a vague guess. 
Turning a hypothesis into a testable prediction (if seasonal, last year should show the same pattern) is good methodology.

4. Partner-level breakdown before mechanism-level hypothesis is the right way to narrow 
the population before you start guessing at causes within it.

Weak points / gaps
1. This is the important one — you may be falling into the same trap you flagged in your churn/reactivation example. 
Ask yourself: does this month's burn draw on this month's earn, or on an accumulated bonus balance built up over previous months? 
If it's a stock, not a same-month flow, then "earn and burn dropped in the same month" 
could be two loosely-related things coinciding — not evidence of one shared upstream cause. 
You'd want to check the lag structure (does last month's earn predict this month's burn?) 
before treating co-movement as proof of a common driver. 
Right now your own framework has a blind spot for the exact pattern you started this conversation with.

2. "Only burn dropped → it's general, not segment-specific" is too strong a rule. 
Low balance could itself be taxi-specific (e.g., taxi's earn rate, minimum redemption threshold,
 or catalog eligibility could differ from other segments). A single-metric move doesn't cleanly separate general vs. segment causes
 — you'd still need to check whether the balance drop is concentrated in taxi users specifically.

3. "Total turnover" is one aggregate number hiding several possible mechanisms. Even if you confirm turnover dropped,
 you don't yet know why: fewer active users, lower trip frequency, or lower average fare each imply different fixes. 
Decompose turnover into active users × frequency × average fare before concluding "activity dropped, case closed."

4. One year of YoY comparison is a thin seasonality test. 
A single prior-year data point can't distinguish real seasonality from last year's own one-off noise. 
Ideally you'd want 2-3 years, or at least check for calendar effects (holiday shifts, weekend count differences)
 rather than relying on a same-month label alone.

5. You don't split supply vs. demand. "Partner turnover dropped" could mean existing partners transacted less 
(demand-side, or partner-specific issue) or that fewer partners were active in the pool at all (supply-side — onboarding/offboarding).
 Same turnover number, different fix.

6. Missing bucket: external/behavioral factors sit between your "turnover" step and your "campaign" step, not after it. 
Competitor promotions, regulatory changes, price changes, or weather could explain a turnover drop without it being seasonal,
 partner-specific, or campaign-related — worth its own checkpoint before you conclude it's seasonality or partner-driven.

7. When you get to campaign analysis, add a counterfactual. "Was it massive or segmented, which channels" is good,
 but you also want a comparison group — users/segments exposed to the campaign change vs. not — and a timing check
(did the metric drop align exactly with when campaign parameters changed), not just a qualitative read of what the campaign looked like.

The core skeleton — activity level → decompose the population → external factors → mechanism — is right.
The main thing I'd fix before using this as a template across your other 9 cases is 
#1: build in an explicit check for whether the two metrics you're comparing are even same-period comparable, 
since that's the exact trap you were describing at the start.
